Where Family Hub fits

Which market this is really in, who else is in it, and what we can honestly claim. Built from four research passes. Every figure carries its source; calculations chaining two sources are flagged; gaps say so rather than guess.

The short answer Sell Family Hub as a kids' money and habits app that needs no bank card. Not a family calendar. Not a learning app. Those are included, and they are good, but they are not why anyone would pay. Lead in the UK, not the Gulf. Ages 6 to 11. The buyer is a parent who already runs pocket money by hand and is tired of it.

01 · The marketWhich market this is really in

Family Hub touches four markets. Only one of them will pay for it.

Market Size, most recent Growth What it means for us
Kids' money and banking $1.8B (2024) 17.2% a year, to $7.2B by 2033 This is the one. Proven revenue, and our price already matches what people pay
Kids' learning apps $7.8B (2024) 13.7% a year Big, but mostly free at entry. A bad place to lead
Family organisers $717M to $1.93B (2026) 8% to 20%, wide spread means low confidence Priced at $3 to $7 a month against free tools. Leading here caps our price
Chore and allowance No separate report exists n/a Not a real market. A feature inside the kids' money apps

Greenlight reported $228.5M of annual revenue in 2024 across 6M+ users. Acorns Early (formerly GoHenry in the US) charges $8 and $12 a month, almost exactly our $7.99 and $12.99. Family organisers charge $3 to $7, so leading as an organiser invites people to compare us at half our price.

02 · The differenceWhat nobody else does

Every competitor found, global and Gulf, ties earning to finishing a chore. Where any of them offer investing, it is a real brokerage account whose value moves with the stock market, disconnected from the child once the money is in.

The finding No competitor was found, anywhere, where an invested balance grows on the days a habit is done and can lose value on the days it is missed. A return that depends on your own consistency rather than the market appears to be genuinely unbuilt. Checked across roughly twenty searches by two independent researchers.

The honest other reading: it may be unbuilt because it does not work. There is no data anywhere on whether shrinking a child's tracked value for a missed habit improves the habit or just breeds resentment. Rooster Money markets "no risk of debt" as a selling point, the opposite instinct. This is the single biggest bet in the product and it is unproven.

They earn twice: a subscription plus a cut of every card swipe. That second income quietly rewards a child spending more, which sits awkwardly beside "teach saving". It also costs them a sponsor bank, identity and anti-money-laundering work, and dispute handling.

In the Gulf specifically

Verity, Leap, Cashee, Edfundo and Savii already run kids' money products in the UAE and Saudi. GEMS and Visa run a live card programme across 200,000+ students in the UAE's largest school network. The space is not empty, and the institutional route is already taken.

04 · The callLaunch in the UK, not the Gulf

The current strategy says Gulf first. The research says otherwise, and it is not close.

Reason Detail
The UK pool is about five times bigger Roughly 8.1M UK families with dependent children against roughly 1.6M UAE households with children. Both chain official statistics, so treat as orders of magnitude
Our price is proven there and untested here $7.99 and $12.99 sit on Acorns Early's $8 and $12 and ClassDojo's $7.99. In the Gulf, Verity and Edfundo publish no price, and Edfundo's own words are "less than the price of a coffee", implying $3 to $5
The words need more care there A balance that grows over time sits close to a concept carrying real weight in a Muslim-majority market, even though ours grows through effort rather than time, which is materially different. A copy decision, not a reason to avoid the market

What would change this call: a Gulf pilot showing families paying at or near $7.99. Gulf smartphone use and cashless sentiment genuinely run ahead of the UK, and education apps there convert over 40% of free trials against about 19% for games. The call is reversible: currency is already per family and localised.

05 · HonestyWhat we can claim today

Drop this claim The positioning document leads with being "the first family app to ask about twins, step-children, grandma". The database has no field for twins, birth order or step-relationships. They exist in a feature document and an architecture decision, and nowhere in the shipped schema. A grandparent, a nanny and a step-parent are all just "adult" today. Leading with a claim the product cannot demonstrate is the fastest way to lose a credible buyer.

Keep this one instead, because it is real and checkable: any number of grown-ups, any number of children, one family space. Verified in the code: five roles, no member cap anywhere, and a sticker rate settable per child.

  • Greenlight caps at 2 adults. Two parents plus a grandparent does not fit.
  • Google Family caps at 6 people. Four children and two parents fills it exactly.
  • Acorns Early caps at 4 children.
  • OurFamilyWizard, what blended families actually use, charges per parent at $110 to $300 a year each, for custody messaging alone.

So the honest story is not "we understand twins". It is "we do not charge you per adult, and we do not run out of seats."

06 · Second buyerSchools are distribution, not revenue

Buyer What they pay Verdict
Homeschool co-ops About $9.95 per family per year We charge $96 to $156. No org pays ten times its own admin software
Schools Wide range, bought through procurement Tenders, year contracts, IT approval. A long sale for one person
The ClassDojo pattern Free for the school This is the one that works. Reached about 95% of US schools, then families pay $7.99 each

Freeze the paid organisation tier. Keep the architecture, it is built and costs nothing to keep. Use organisations later as a cheap way to reach many paying families at once, on the normal consumer subscription.

07 · PositionThe words to actually use

The line Pocket money that teaches saving, not just spending. No card, no bank, no risk.

A child earns stickers by keeping habits. The family decides what a sticker is worth. The child can spend them in the family's own reward shop, put them safe in savings, or put them behind a habit and watch the value grow while they keep it up. No card is issued. No real money moves through us.

The limit to state out loud: this works for roughly ages 5 to 12, where the lesson is behavioural. For teenagers who need to actually hold and spend a balance, a real card wins, and we should not pretend otherwise. Saying so is more credible than claiming the whole age range.
